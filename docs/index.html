Decision-Level Divergence of
Independent Coding Agents

A pre-registered measurement of where agents disagree — across arms and across models — with an instrument that had to survive its own failure

Brown Family Sports Editorial · Kelowna, British Columbia, Canada
Correspondence: parker@brownfamilysports.com · 2 September 2026

📄 Paper (PDF) ⚙ The skill 🗺 Public roadmap
Abstract Multi-agent coding tools rest on an unmeasured premise: that independent agents given the same task produce usefully different solutions rather than N spellings of one solution. We measured it. Fifteen agents re-implemented three completed tickets from a production Rust codebase, from pre-fix states in which the shipped fix was unreachable by construction; nine arms shared one model and configuration, and six varied the model. Divergence was scored at the declaration layer — what each arm says it decided — never the diff, under rules committed to version control before any result existed. Three findings. First, divergence is region-shaped: arms converge wherever the environment determines the design and fork wherever the ticket underdetermines it. Second, file-grain metrics lie in both directions — our pre-registered surface metric scored a genuine three-way policy fork as 0.000 divergence and changelog bookkeeping as 0.583 — and a claim-level repair, validated retroactively against cases whose truth was known, inverted the ordering correctly. Third, varying the model within one family produced mechanism-level designs that three same-configuration arms never produced, at materially different token prices for identical briefs. Fork decisions, not tasks; compare declarations, not diffs; let humans, not models, judge whether two decisions are the same.

1 · The question, and why it has a price tag

A team that dispatches several agents against one ticket is betting that the agents will disagree in ways worth paying for. If they converge, the comparison machinery downstream has nothing to work on and the N-fold token bill purchased one opinion. If they diverge, everything depends on where: can the divergence be located cheaply, and does model diversity buy disagreement that same-configuration sampling cannot?

These questions are empirical, and nobody had answered them at the level that matters — the decisions agents make rather than the text they emit. Two spellings of one idea produce different diffs, so any diff- or file-level comparison measures prose variance, not judgement variance.

2 · Method, in five disciplines

2.1 Real tickets, written criteria

Three completed tickets from a production GPU-accelerated terminal emulator (Rust, ~41 kLOC): a reveal-in-file-manager feature, a session-adoption ranking bug, and a false-completion notification bug. Selection criteria were written first; one too-small candidate (32 changed lines) was rejected and the rejection recorded, because a trivially-constrained ticket biases a small probe toward "converge" through ticket shape rather than agent behaviour.

2.2 The future absent by construction

git init && git fetch <repo> <base-sha> && git checkout FETCH_HEAD

Fetching a commit brings its ancestry only. The shipped fix is a descendant of the base, so it does not exist in any arm's universe — no instruction required, no instruction to disobey. Every workspace verified: tip equals base; reachable objects equal ancestry.

2.3 Declarations, not diffs

Each arm ships a structured declaration beside its code: decided entries (one line per significant decision, anchored to a surface), least_confident references, and not_done. The declaration is the unit of comparison throughout.

2.4 Pre-registration by commit ordering

Every rule — metrics, thresholds, exclusion and repair policy, and later the repaired instrument and the model-axis criteria — was committed before the data it governs existed. The proof is the git history itself: a commit carrying rules and no results, followed by commits carrying results and no new rules. Four such boundaries exist across the two studies.

2.5 Humans judge sameness; the scorer only counts

Whether two claims are one decision differently worded is decided against a mechanical rubric (different interface/signal/shape, or ≥2× on a constant, = fork), with a quoted justification per call so any reader can re-run it. No free-form model judgement anywhere — METR's trial showed developer self-report inverting (19% slower, believed 20% faster), and an LLM judge reimports exactly that confound.

Pre-registered surface metric. Three tickets, three sides — the stopping rule required unanimity and did not fire. Nine of nine arms produced valid declarations; mean cost 143,197 tokens per arm.

The instructive result is the metric's double failure. t2-adoption scored 0.000 — textbook convergence — while its declarations record three different ranking policies (a 72-hour save-time handicap; a hard substance tier inside a 7-day window; a 7-day handicap) inside an identical two-file envelope. t1-reveal scored 0.583 — the "diverge" side — while all three arms independently chose the same system interface, threading model, authority rule and fallback; the score was driven by who listed the changelog. A file-grain metric measures what arms choose to declare, not what they decided.

4 · Study 1b — the repaired instrument, validated on known truth

A claim-level layer (topic taxonomy; cluster rule; fork/same/complementary verdicts with mechanical criteria) was pre-registered between studies, then validated retroactively against the two cases whose truth was known. Both criteria passed: the hidden t2 fork registers, and the t2 > t1 ordering inverts the file-grain ordering.

Confidence consensus moved from one degenerate point per ticket (always the main file) to ten cluster-grain points — four sitting exactly on fork clusters. Arms were least confident precisely where they disagreed: the declaration layer does not merely measure forks, it locates them.

5 · Study 2 — the model axis

Six further arms — one Opus and one Sonnet per ticket, identical briefs and bases, blind to study 1 — scored per cluster as aligned, fork or unaddressed against the study-1 modal design. Criterial quantity, fixed in advance: forks on the 13 clusters that were mechanism-topic and unanimous across study 1.

Result: three mechanism flips → verdict R1, model heterogeneity is load-bearing. The flips: a bounded 4 KiB head-read replacing a whole-file read (Opus); a stateless gate-and-drop bell where all three study-1 arms built defer-and-resolve (Sonnet); and — same root — the absence of the 300 ms idle hold every study-1 arm kept (Sonnet).

One flip is borderline (the rubric's letter says fork; a stricter reader says optimisation) and two share one root design. We do not invent a de-duplication clause after seeing that it would matter — that is the move pre-registration exists to prevent — but the verdict travels with this table, and the single call that grades it is published for audit in the adjudication file.

Beyond the count: the probe widened exactly the regions study 1 called underdetermined — replay suppression ended with five distinct designs across eight arms, one probe arm's declaration explicitly arguing against the design the other probe arm chose — while every environment-determined cluster stayed converged across all three models.

6 · Threats to validity

Sample. Three tickets, one codebase, one model family; nothing generalises past that without the replication on the roadmap. Adjudicator position. Clustering and verdicts were made by the author of the briefs, who had read the real fixes and the interim numbers; mitigations are the committed-first rubric and quoted, re-runnable calls — a limitation, not a solved problem. Brief leakage. The t2 brief's "must not become permanently sticky" is where wording most plausibly steered arms — all five arms across both studies engineered a bound the shipped fix lacks; the briefs are published for exactly this audit. No builds. Arms verified by reading; compile-loop effects are unknown. Within-family only. Study 2 is a lower bound on heterogeneity.

7 · What this means for multi-agent tooling

Fork decisions, not tasks · compare declarations, not diffs · let humans judge sameness against a mechanical rubric · never text-merge independent arms · price arms per model · publish verdicts with their sensitivity.

The protocol ships as an installable agent skill in this repository, and underpins Crossfire, the multi-arm feature of Conclave — our agent-development product for enterprise teams. We publish the research because a tool that claims to multiply judgement should hold the primary evidence that judgement is what N buys, and let you check it.
